Electricity & Magnetism:
Make the Invisible Visible

Build a circuit, align magnetic domains, switch on an electromagnet, spin a motor and generate electricity. Predict first. Experiment. Watch what changes.

Teacher note: These are simplified visual models. Field lines and moving particles are representations, not literal threads or exact atomic-scale pictures. Use only safe low-voltage classroom equipment—never mains electricity.

Mission 1 — Close the path!

Electric current needs a continuous conducting path. Predict, then flip the switch.

Open path: sustained current stops and the bulb is off.

Think like a scientist

Why does the bulb go out when the switch opens?

A bulb transfers electrical energy to light and heat; it does not consume current.

Mission 2 — Inside a magnet

Explore a simplified magnetic-domain model: many tiny magnetic regions can be randomly oriented or more aligned.

Observe: mixed directions mostly cancel at the material's scale.

What does the model show?

Ferromagnetic materials contain magnetic domains, regions in which many atomic magnetic moments are aligned. In an unmagnetised piece, domain directions are mixed; magnetising can align more domains.

Mission 3 — Map the invisible field

Turn current on, move the compass around a wire, then reverse current.

Current OFF: switch on to see the magnetic field pattern.

History bite: Ørsted, 1820

Hans Christian Ørsted observed that an electric current could deflect a nearby compass needle. This helped establish a link between electricity and magnetism.

What happens to the field direction when current reverses?

Field lines show the pattern and direction; they are not physical threads.

Mission 4 — Build an electromagnet

Pass current through a coil around an iron core. Change one variable at a time to compare strength.

Ready: increase turns or current and compare.

Make a fair test

Keep current fixed while changing turns. Then keep turns fixed while changing current. Both can affect the strength of an electromagnet.

This is an illustrative indicator, not a calibrated field measurement. An electromagnet can be switched off; a permanent magnet does not require current to maintain its field.

Mission 5 — Make a motor spin

A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences forces. Forces on opposite sides create a turning effect.

Stopped: start, then change current or poles.

Why does it rotate?

Magnetic forces act on the current-carrying sides of the coil in opposite directions, creating torque. A real DC motor uses a commutator to reverse coil current every half-turn so the rotation continues.

This animation illustrates the turning effect; the rotating coil is simplified and not a full engineering model.

Mission 6 — Generate electricity

Move a magnet relative to a coil. Changing magnetic conditions can induce voltage.

Idle: try moving the magnet. Faster motion gives a larger induced signal in this simplified model.
Illustrative induced signal: 0

History bite: Faraday, 1831

Michael Faraday demonstrated electromagnetic induction. A changing magnetic field through a coil can induce voltage; with a closed circuit, that voltage can drive current.

Energy conversion: generator — mechanical to electrical; motor — electrical to mechanical.

Mission 7 — Science has a story

Use these short history stops to launch discussion and connect discovery to each simulation.

c. 1600
William Gilbert

His book De Magnete argued that Earth behaves like a giant magnet.

1820
Hans Christian Ørsted

A current-carrying wire deflected a nearby compass, linking electricity and magnetism.

1825
William Sturgeon

He made an early practical electromagnet using a coil and iron core.

1831
Michael Faraday

He demonstrated electromagnetic induction—the basis of generator technology.

1800s
Electric motors develop

Many inventors developed practical motors during the 19th century; the core idea is magnetic force creating motion.

Today
Everyday technology

Motors, generators, speakers, relays and power systems use the link between electricity and magnetism.

Suggested teacher story arc

  1. Start with a compass: “Why does it point consistently north–south?”
  2. Show Ørsted: “Can electricity affect a magnet?”
  3. Build an electromagnet: “Can we switch magnetism on and off?”
  4. Run the motor: “Can magnetic force create motion?”
  5. Run the generator: “Can motion create electricity?”

History is a set of milestones, not a complete list of all contributors.
